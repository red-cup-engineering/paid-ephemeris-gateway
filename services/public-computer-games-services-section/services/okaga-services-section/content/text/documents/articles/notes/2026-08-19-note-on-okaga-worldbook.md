> "Picture quality narrative meets Discworld MUD meets EVE." As hard scifi as it can be, with
> exactly one conceit: some people can channel energy from outside the dimensions of this world
> to do warp travel and other forms of accelerating the arrow of time. Ships otherwise reach only
> ~0.2c. A galactic confederacy. A few billion players shouldn't skew galactic economics.
> — the commission, owner, 2026-08-19

This is the setting bible for that commission. It is built to be **read once for the shape of the
world** and **eaten forever after by a storylet compiler** — §6's gazetteer entries are written as
literal, schema-consistent blocks for that purpose. Everything in it traces to one of two things:
the thin but real Okaga canon already in this repo, or the physics of laser-pushed light sails at
0.1–0.2c. Where it invents beyond both, it says so, in the margin, every time.

## 1. The physics baseline: sail, array, light-lag

### 1.1 The ceiling, and why it's a wall, not a rule

*(HOUSE SUBSTRATE, from the physics constraint sheet, OWNER CANON numbers)*

Every crewed hull in the Confederacy is a photonic sail: a thin, engineered-reflective membrane
kilometers across, kicked to speed by a phased-array laser installation in its home system and,
critically, **caught** by a matching array at the destination. Top practical speed sits at
0.1–0.2c, matching the owner's own numbers across two different years of notes. This is not an
arbitrary limit — kinetic energy scales with v², so every doubling of transit speed costs roughly
four times the delivered beam energy (more once relativistic corrections bite), and a
crewed hull — life support, radiation shielding, cargo, people, not a gram-scale probe — sits
much further down that energy curve than an unmanned relativistic probe design ever could. Above
roughly 0.2c, the beam power, the array size, and the sail's own thermal load all become
unworkable for anything with real mass. **The ceiling is an economics problem, and that fact is
itself load-bearing**: it means faster travel is always, in principle, purchasable — with enough
beam power, enough array, enough energy — which is exactly the door §2's one conceit walks
through.

Deceleration is the harder engineering problem, and it is where the owner's own 2019 log already
gives the correct real-world answer without knowing it: arrays "aimed out toward other systems"
means **every settled system's array launches its own outbound ships and catches everyone else's
inbound ones**, decelerating an arriving hull symmetrically to how the origin array accelerated
it. This is the only known plausible way a crewed ship going 0.15–0.2c stops at all (the
alternative — magnetic-sail braking against the interstellar medium — only works over 50–100-year
timescales, useful as a slow backup for damaged-array arrivals, never as the everyday method).

**Relativity at 0.2c is real but modest** — γ ≈ 1.021, about 2% time dilation. A ten-year transit
is a ten-year transit for everyone aboard, give or take months, not decades. This matters
enormously for craft discipline: Skah's twelve real years aboard HCS Okaga (OWNER CANON) are not
a relativistic trick and this worldbook never treats them as one. Any felt time-skew in this
setting is either sociological (isolation, routine, the length of a voyage) or, once §2 exists,
literally purchased — never a free byproduct of going fast.

### 1.2 The Current: arteries and chokepoints

*(term OWNER-KEPT; network model NEW CANON PROPOSED)*

The Confederacy's settled worlds are not spread at average galactic-field density (roughly one
star per 250–300 cubic light-years near the Confederacy's core, average neighbor spacing ~4 ly).
Corridors were never built in straight lines to the "best" destination — they were built hop by
buildable hop, and a hop is only buildable if the two arrays at either end can actually catch each
other's beam at their aperture and power budget. **Corridors follow the cheapest available chain
of stars, not the shortest path between two points**, which means routes in active service run
noticeably *denser* than the galactic field average — commonly ~2 ly hop-to-hop along an
established line. This is the arithmetic under HCS Okaga's own "about a decade" between systems:
2 ly ÷ 0.2c ≈ 10 years. It also reconciles a highway with off-ramps: systems not on any built
corridor exist inside the same volume, reachable only by long, expensive, high-risk direct hops —
this is where frontier settlement and piracy both live.

This network — the sum of every launch-and-catch array and the beam-paths between them — is
**the Current**. It is the Confederacy's only fast channel for goods, people, *and* information,
because the arrays are the comms backbone too (OWNER CANON, §0.4). A system's place in the
Confederacy is entirely a function of its position in this graph:

- **Hub systems** — multiple arrays, multiple live routes, redundant. Losing one link doesn't cut
  them off. These are the Core Worlds (§4).
- **Spur systems** — one array, one route in, one route out. A single act of sabotage — exactly
  what happens to Ben in "The Okaga" — doesn't just strand the ship in orbit, it silences the
  *system*: no outbound signal, no inbound signal, nothing until either the array is rebuilt or a
  patrol ship happens by. Ben is a spur system by this model, and its plot is the spur-system
  failure mode played out in full.
- **Dark space** — everything off the Current entirely. Reachable, if at all, only the hard way.

### 1.3 Light-lag governance — the Foundation problem, named plainly

*(HOUSE SUBSTRATE, physics constraint sheet)*

A signal at c between two Current-linked systems takes years to decades one-way, full stop — no
trick reduces that for anyone who isn't the one traveling. A ship crossing the same gap at 0.2c
takes roughly five times that long in its own barely-dilated frame. The practical consequence,
stated as plainly as the setting should ever state it: **a central government separated from its
own periphery by decades of light-lag cannot govern that periphery in the ordinary sense.** It
cannot respond to a crisis before the crisis has already resolved itself one way or another. It
can only set slow-moving default law, arbitrate retroactively, and periodically re-survey.

This is not a new idea layered onto Okaga — it is the plot the owner already wrote. "The Okaga"'s
Act 3 has Ben's marooned population solve a monoculture blight by applying "the confederacy's
means of organization on a smaller level," decades before any ship could plausibly help, and the
story's own closing tension is whether the Confederacy's central government does any good at all
by trying to administer such places versus simply extracting value from them once it finally
arrives. **That is Foundation's actual premise, transplanted whole, already sitting in the source
material.** This worldbook's job is to name it as the setting's operating logic and build every
institution in §5 and §6 to match it — nothing here needs to invent the light-lag problem, only
to take it seriously everywhere at once.

What such a Confederacy *can* coordinate: shared protocols fixed in advance (ship design, array
specification, a common trade tongue, measurement standards), slow custom-and-precedent law, and
periodic census-and-arbitration visits — the Patrol circuit (§6.2), exactly as canon already has
it. What it cannot coordinate: real-time markets, live diplomacy, synchronized elections, timely
disaster response, or any notion of a single "current state" shared by more than one system at a
time.

### 1.4 The boundary, stated once, before §2 breaks it on purpose

Everything above this line is ordinary physics: momentum, energy, c as a hard ceiling, light-lag
as an unbreakable political fact. **The one exception the commission asks for lives entirely in
§2, and nowhere else.** No other technology in this worldbook is permitted to quietly relax any of
the above. If a future storylet needs faster information, cheaper deceleration, or a shortcut
around light-lag, the answer is always: buy it from a channeler, pay the price in §2, or don't get
it.

## 2. The one conceit, priced

*(NEW CANON PROPOSED throughout, worked out as the diegetic form of the house's
money=energy=time law, §0 item 5)*

> "Money is energy is time" is not a slogan here. It is a conservation law with an accountant.

### 2.1 The Fold — why it's rare

A small fraction of people are born with a neuro-developmental trait informally called **the
Fold**: a structural quirk that lets a trained nervous system open a stable, narrow aperture onto
**the Outside** — a reservoir beyond the world's four dimensions, inert and unreachable to
everyone else. The Fold is not a skill; it cannot be taught into someone who lacks it, only found.
Estimated incidence: on the order of 1 in 40,000 births. Of those, most Folds are too weak to hold
an aperture open under any load, and a meaningful fraction of the rest do not survive supervised
training (early, unsupervised channeling is close to always fatal — see §2.5). Net result: roughly
1 licensed, working **Channeler** per 700,000–800,000 population, confederacy-wide. Against a
total population in the trillions (§4), this is a genuinely small, genuinely precious labor pool —
which is exactly why it is priced the way §2.7 prices it, and exactly why it is institutionally
guarded rather than left to individuals (§6.2).

### 2.2 The loom — why it needs a building

An aperture opened unassisted collapses in seconds and takes the channeler's nervous system with
it. Every legal channeling event happens inside a **loom**: a purpose-built stabilizing cradle,
expensive, stationary or ship-mounted at significant mass and power cost, that contains the
aperture, meters the draw, and — critically — makes the transaction detectable (§2.4). Looms are
rare infrastructure by design: a handful per Core World, fewer per Established Colony, essentially
none on the Frontier. This gives channeled transport the same texture as the Current's own
chokepoints — a small number of high-value nodes that concentrate both political power and
banditry risk (§6.2's Counting House; the setting's pirates target loom convoys, not just cargo
holds).

### 2.3 What channeling buys — two levers, both bounded by relativity

A channeler at a loom spends drawn energy — **chron**, unit χ — on exactly two things, and the
one conceit is deliberately narrow: it never relaxes c, it only pays to approach it or to
compress subjective time within it.

- **Push** — a direct momentum gift to a hull beyond what its sail's beam budget bought it. Cost
  scales with the *increase* in relativistic kinetic energy demanded, same v²-and-worse curve as
  §1.1 — meaning Push is cheapest as a top-up on an already sail-boosted ship, never a
  replacement for launch infrastructure. A well-funded Push can carry a small, loom-rated hull up
  toward 0.6–0.9c for a single priority run. **It never reaches or exceeds c.** This is "warp
  travel" as the commission asked for it: real, priced, dangerous, and still causally sound.
- **Skip** — accelerating the local arrow of time inside the loom's containment field, for
  whatever mass sits within it. The ship still crosses the gap at whatever speed it's actually
  going, bounded by c like everything else — but the people and cargo inside the field experience
  fewer subjective years for the same span of galactic-frame time. Cost scales with mass in the
  field × the compression ratio × the duration compressed. This is "accelerating the arrow of
  time" exactly as the commission phrased it, and it is the lever that produces the setting's
  Hyperion texture (§5.3) without ever touching causality: **the news a Skip-traveler carries
  still only arrives when their ship physically does.** No channeling event, of either kind, ever
  transmits information faster than a physical arrival. This is the hard rule that keeps §1.3's
  entire governance model intact under the one conceit — stated once here, binding everywhere.

### 2.4 Conservation accounting, part one — the corona

Energy does not appear from nothing inside the four dimensions, even when its ultimate source is
Outside. The instant an aperture opens, the loom must source a matching quantity from within the
world to balance the books locally, and it does so by drawing down the **local flow-rate of time
itself** in a bounded shell around the loom — a **corona**: a physically real, instrumented,
measurable region of clock-rate anomaly surrounding every channeling event, proportional to the χ
drawn, for the duration of the transaction. This is the *immediate*, in-dimension half of the
conservation law — what the channeled energy displaces, right now, is the passage of time in the
volume around the transaction. It is also, deliberately, a detection mechanic: sanctioned looms
are shielded and registered; unlicensed or smuggled channeling still throws a corona, and any
station or patrol ship with a chronometer array can see one forming. This is the setting's
built-in cat-and-mouse hook — smugglers try to mask a corona's signature; the Counting House
(§6.2) exists partly to police it.

### 2.5 Conservation accounting, part two — the Toll

The corona only balances the *local* books. What's actually owed back across the aperture, to the
Outside, is time — and it is collected, deferred, in two separate ledgers.

The first is biological, and it falls on the channeler alone: every χ drawn measurably ages the
channeler's own tissue, faster than the calendar around them. A veteran who has run a career's
worth of Push and Skip jobs looks decades older than their birth-year says — grounded, retired
channelers are a setting-standard image: chronologically young, calendar-young even, and visibly,
irreversibly old. This is **the Toll**, and it is why the Fold, already rare, produces an even
smaller pool of *career* channelers: the trait is common enough to be found regularly; surviving
a working life on it is not.

### 2.6 Conservation accounting, part three — time-debt

The second ledger falls on whoever was carried, and it is the setting's Hyperion mechanic made
literal rather than metaphorical: a fraction of every Skip's temporal displacement is charged
against the *traveler's own worldline*, not just the channeler's body, as **time-debt** — a
standing, portable, checkable number, tracked by the Counting House against the traveler's
identity, not their purse. Debt is not repaid with money. It is repaid by **settling**: living out
ordinary, uncompressed time, proportional to the debt owed, during which the debtor cannot
legally book another Skip. An unpaid debt does not sit quietly — left to accrue past a threshold,
it collects itself involuntarily, in a **snap**: sudden, catastrophic correction, most often
instant aging equivalent to the unpaid years (sometimes fatal), rarest and worst, a traveler found
alive but existing measurably out of phase with everyone's clock but their own — reachable,
present, and unaging until the debt resolves on its own schedule, not theirs.

This is the mechanism behind the commission's own request, almost verbatim: *the person you left
returns older, or not at all.* A pilgrim who Skips to reach a dying relative in time arrives
having spent weeks where their family spent years — and by the time their own debt is settled,
either by choice or by force, the people who didn't travel have simply kept living. Sometimes
there is no one left to go home to. Sometimes going home at all means booking another Skip, adding
debt they cannot afford, and choosing not to.

### 2.7 The chron economy — the literal purchase of time

Ordinary Confederate commerce runs on **credits** (already house canon, §0 item 5). Chron is a
separate, Counting-House-tracked quantity, bought with credits at a posted, heavily regulated,
constantly political exchange rate — **whoever sets that rate controls who, in practice, is
allowed to buy time**, and that fact alone is enough political fuel for a hundred storylets
without needing anything else invented. The purchase chain is exactly the sentence the house
already wrote before this worldbook existed: *credits buy chron, chron buys a channeler's draw
from the Outside, the draw buys Push or Skip.* A channeler moving a ship faster than its sail, or
a family buying their elder a few extra years of witnessed life by Skipping a grandchild home
early, is not a metaphor for purchasing time. It is a line item.

### 2.8 The hard boundary, restated

No channeling event ever exceeds c. No channeling event ever transmits information faster than
its own physical arrival. Every χ drawn is repaid, in time, by someone — the channeler's body, the
traveler's worldline, or both. **These three sentences are load-bearing** (see §8) — everything
else about the conceit's flavor (chron, corona, Toll, snap, the Counting House) is free to be
renamed or reshaped without breaking the setting underneath it.

## 3. What "Okaga" names

*(NEW CANON PROPOSED, built to not touch the one hard fact in OWNER CANON: HCS Okaga is a
specific, proper-named ship.)*

The word already has exactly one fixed meaning: the proper name of a particular vessel, the
*Human-Confederacy Ship Okaga*, Lakota-named, dinner-plate-and-rotating-tube, currently running
the Ben circuit in the owner's own draft. This worldbook does not touch that.

**Proposed extension, for the game to have a title worth wearing:** "Okaga" becomes, by slow
informal drift among the settled worlds, the common noun for the *class* of vessel HCS Okaga
belongs to — colony-and-patrol hulls built to the same dinner-plate-sail-and-tube pattern, running
the same Arrival Convention circuit, the way "a zeppelin" outlived being only *the* Zeppelin.
Citizens say "an Okaga is coming" the way they'd say "a patrol ship," and *the* Okaga — HCS
Okaga specifically — remains a famous, individually-named example of her own class, exactly as
canon already has her. This gives players a fleet of ships to crew, name, and lose, rather than
one irreplaceable protagonist vessel, without overwriting a single sentence of what's already
written about her.

## 4. Scale math: why a few billion players can't skew a galactic economy

*(NEW CANON PROPOSED — the numbers; the method is the physics sheet's own math applied
outward.)*

### 4.1 How big the settled volume is, and why

Stellar density near a system like the Confederacy's core runs roughly 0.004 stars per cubic
light-year (about one star per 250 ly³, consistent with the commonly-cited ~4 ly average nearest-
neighbor spacing). A sphere of radius 250 ly around the home system therefore contains, by the
volume formula (4/3)πr³:

```
(4/3) × π × 250³ × 0.004 ≈ 261,800 stars
```

If roughly 2% of stars in that volume host a colonizable system — generous by any real
habitability estimate, but this is a settled premise of the fiction, not a claim about our own
galaxy — that's **~5,000 settled systems**, close enough to round to a working number: **5,150**.
At an effective frontier-expansion rate well under the ship ceiling (§1.1's economics wall applies
doubly to *building* new Current infrastructure, not just running it — call it ~0.05c average,
accounting for the decades of dwell-and-build time between hops), a 250 ly radius implies a
Confederacy on the order of **several thousand years old** — comfortably matching the owner's own
framing of Okaga as "a far-off future" setting.

The Confederacy's settled volume, at ~500 ly across, is a *regional bubble*, not the whole Milky
Way (~100,000 ly across) — citizens call it "the Settled Sky" and use "galactic" the way people
once used "the world" for the known Mediterranean: aspirational, not literal. This matters for
§1.3: light-lag corner-to-corner across even a 500 ly bubble is centuries, which is already more
than enough to make real-time central governance impossible — the Confederacy does not need to
claim the whole galaxy to have a genuine Foundation problem.

### 4.2 Population

Three tiers, matched to the Current's own topology (§1.2):

| Tier | Systems | Avg. population/system | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core Worlds (hub systems, long-settled, multi-body population like Ben's own three-body spread) | 150 | 12 billion | 1.8 trillion |
| Established Colonies (regular Patrol circuit, Ben's own tier) | 1,000 | 1.8 billion | 1.8 trillion |
| Frontier settlements (spur or dark-space, fragile, Rimworld-scale) | 4,000 | 100 million | 0.4 trillion |
| **Total** | **5,150** | — | **~4.0 trillion** |

### 4.3 The player fraction

The commission's own arithmetic: world population today is ~8.1–8.2 billion; half is ~4.05–4.1
billion — call it **4 billion players**.

```
4,000,000,000 / 4,000,000,000,000 = 0.001 = 0.1%
```

**One person in a thousand.** Even generously overweighting player economic footprint 10–50×
against an average background citizen — players are protagonists, not background population, and
should transact, travel, and fight far more than an NPC baseline — the aggregate player share of
Confederacy-wide economic activity stays in the low single digits at worst, nowhere near enough to
move a *galactic* aggregate.

### 4.4 The independent argument: it wouldn't matter if it were bigger

The population-fraction argument above is the structural one. §1.3 supplies a second, mechanical
one that holds even if the player population were ten times larger: **no actor, player or
otherwise, can coordinate economic action faster than light-lag allows.** A coalition of players
spread across a thousand systems cannot arbitrage a galaxy-spanning market in real time for the
same reason the Confederacy's own government cannot govern one in real time — the information
needed to coordinate the action is, structurally, decades to centuries stale by the time it
arrives anywhere useful. Player economies are real, local, and consequential *at system and
corridor scale*, exactly where Rimworld-style stakes belong (§5.1) — and structurally incapable of
aggregating into a "galactic" event, exactly where Foundation-style limits belong (§5.2). The two
arguments converge on purpose: population makes it *improbable*, physics makes it *impossible*.

## 5. Three pillars, three scales

### 5.1 Rimworld — the local scale

*(Carried almost entirely by existing OWNER CANON.)* Fragile settlements, material survival,
personalities mattering — this is already "The Okaga"'s own texture: a blight that could be
solved by antibiotics the Okaga happens to be carrying (Dapu, Scene 3); a requisition system
counted in individual seed envelopes (Skah, Scene 2); a named Union with an armband and a serial
number, not an abstract "faction." Frontier settlements (§4.2's 4,000-system tier) are where this
plays hardest: single-array chokepoints (§1.2), no real prospect of timely outside help (§1.3),
survival riding on decisions made by named people, not institutions. Design implication for
storylets: Frontier-tier content should almost never involve the Counting House or the Patrol
arriving in time to matter — the local cast has to solve it, or not, before help is even in
transit.

### 5.2 Foundation — the institutional scale

*(HOUSE SUBSTRATE, §1.3, already the shape of the owner's own plot.)* Predictive institutes,
decay and renewal across light-lag. This worldbook proposes two standing institutions carrying it
(full entries in §6.2): the **Patrol**, whose entire mode of operation *is* "arrive decades late,
arbitrate retroactively, leave" — canon's "galactic patrol" formalized; and the **Counting
House**, the Confederacy's chron-licensing and debt-ledger authority, which is Foundation's
"opaque central power that everyone depends on and no one elected" reimagined through the §2
economy instead of through predictive mathematics. The live political question this worldbook
deliberately leaves open for storylets, because "The Okaga" itself leaves it open: is the
Confederacy's slow governance actually good for the worlds it's meant to serve, or is it a system
that shows up once a generation mainly to extract value from what people built without it?

### 5.3 Hyperion — the player scale

*(Carried entirely by §2.6–2.7.)* Pilgrimage structure and time-debt tragedy against vast
intervals. The Arrival Convention (already OWNER CANON) is already a pilgrimage structure — a
system's delegates converge on a ship on a schedule none of them set. §2 gives it a second,
sharper form: the **Channeled Pilgrimage**, a deliberate Skip booked to reach a specific person or
event in calendar time the traveler could never otherwise make — a wedding, a deathbed, a verdict
— purchased in credits, paid in the traveler's own future years, occasionally paid in full at
once, involuntarily, as a snap. This is where the setting's personal stories live: not empires
rising and falling, but one person deciding whether the years they'd owe are worth the years they'd
get to witness.

## 7. House style, kept from the owner's own notes

*(OWNER-KEPT, from the 2023 transcript's editorial guidance — worth carrying forward as writing
guidance for any storylet text, not just worldbuilding fact.)*

> "My writing does not go out of its way to explain the fictional people, places, or technologies
> it introduces." / "Focus on crafting short scenes with tangible stakes... subtle hints at larger
> world-building elements without directly explaining them."

Practically: storylet and NPC text should use *chron*, *the Fold*, *a snap*, *the Current* the way
"The Okaga" already uses "immersim" and "REQ" — unglossed, in motion, trusted to the reader. This
worldbook itself is the explainer; the fiction it feeds should almost never be.

## 8. Load-bearing vs. flavor

| Element | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Dual-use signalling arrays (propulsion + comms) | **Load-bearing** | OWNER CANON; the entire light-lag/governance model depends on it |
| 0.1–0.2c sail ceiling as an energy-economics wall | **Load-bearing** | OWNER CANON numbers; everything in §2 is priced *against* this ceiling |
| Light-lag defeats real-time central governance | **Load-bearing** | Already the shape of "The Okaga"'s own Act 3 |
| Channeling never exceeds c; never transmits info faster than physical arrival | **Load-bearing** | The one conceit's entire discipline — remove this and §1.3 collapses |
| Toll (channeler cost) + time-debt (traveler cost) as two separate ledgers | **Load-bearing** | This is what makes the conceit "priced" rather than free magic |
| Corona detectability | **Load-bearing** | The conservation accounting the commission explicitly asked for |
| Exact figures (5,150 systems, 4.0 trillion population, 250 ly radius) | **Flavor** | Illustrative and self-consistent, not fixed truths — adjust freely, keep the *ratios* (§4.3's 0.1%) if you do |
| Terminology (chron, the Fold, the loom, the Counting House, snap) | **Flavor** | Renameable without touching mechanics |
| "The Current," "Thalassa Station," ship names (*Sable Antelope*, *Peregrine*) | **Flavor** | OWNER-KEPT tone material, not obligatory |
| Okaga as a class name, not just one ship | **Flavor, but see §9** | A naming proposal, not a mechanic — easy to keep or drop |
| Character names (Skah, Hotah, Dapu) | **Canon-fixed as characters**, not load-bearing as game mechanics | They belong to "The Okaga" specifically; usable as historical/ancestor figures, not mandatory NPCs |

## 9. The three boldest proposals

1. **§2, the full chron/Toll/time-debt/corona system.** The single biggest invention in the
   document and the one everything else leans on. A gentler or differently-flavored conceit (no
   visible-aging body horror, no involuntary "snap" events) is a rewrite of §2.4–2.6 specifically —
   §1's physics baseline and §4's scale math don't depend on the flavor, only on the three
   load-bearing sentences in §2.8.
2. **§4, the Confederacy as a ~500 ly regional bubble, not the whole galaxy.** "Galactic
   confederacy" and "EVE-scale" might suggest a bigger claim than this document makes. A regional
   bubble is *more* defensible (real stellar density, a checkable light-lag argument) and still
   enormous relative to any character — but it is a genuine scope decision, not a neutral default.
3. **§3, "Okaga" as a ship class, not only the one ship.** Touches the owner's own proper noun
   directly. Built to not contradict a single existing sentence about HCS Okaga specifically, but
   it is still a reinterpretation of what the word covers.
